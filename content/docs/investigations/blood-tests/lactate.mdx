# Lactate

- Lactate is formed when glucose is broken down anaerobically.
- It regenerates NAD+ from NADH, allowing glycolysis to continue producing ATP in the absence of oxygen.
- RBCs rely entirely on this pathway since they lack mitochondria.

## Clinical Significance

- **Normal range**: 1-2 mmol/L
- **Exercise peak**: Can rise > 20 mmol/L
- **Dangerous elevation**: Persistent > 4 mmol/L (is associated with increased mortality in critically ill patients.)

## Causes of Elevated Lactate

### Type A (Hypoxic) Lactic Acidosis

- Caused by tissue hypoxia, leading to anaerobic metabolism.
- Common causes include:
  1. Shock (septic, cardiogenic, hypovolemic)
  1. Severe anemia
  1. Respiratory failure
  1. Carbon monoxide poisoning

### Type B (Non-Hypoxic) Lactic Acidosis

- Caused by metabolic disturbances or toxins, not directly related to oxygen delivery.
- Lactate is cleared by the liver and kidneys, so dysfunction in these organs can lead to accumulation.
- Common causes include:
  1. Liver failure
  1. Renal failure
  1. Drug-induced (e.g., metformin, linezolid, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors such as zidovudine, stavudine, didanosine)
  1. Inborn errors of metabolism (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency)
